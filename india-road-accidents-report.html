Why did 2-wheeler accidents sharply spike in 2009?

Chart coming soon — will visualize two-wheeler sales & fleet growth vs. fatalities, plus age/gender skew of victims

At first glance, the jump in two-wheeler fatalities in 2009 looks like a sudden surge in risk on India's roads. But the fleet growth numbers don't quite support a "more bikes, more accidents" story: 2008–09 sat in the middle of the global financial crisis, and two-wheeler sales growth in India slowed to roughly 2.6% that year — hardly the kind of fleet expansion that would explain a spike of this size.

A more likely explanation is a shift in how accidents were being classified and recorded around that period, rather than a genuine one-year surge in risk. It's also worth noting that NCRB data has consistently shown young men as the dominant fatality group among two-wheeler riders — a pattern that ties into who was riding, and how much exposure they had, even before 2009.

Why does Tamil Nadu have the highest cumulative deaths despite being less populous than UP/Maharashtra?

Tamil Nadu's road toll is best explained by a combination of two factors rather than either one alone: an above-average, dense road network, and the second-largest vehicle fleet of any Indian state. Road density by itself doesn't explain it — that's actually higher in Bihar and West Bengal — but paired with a huge and growing number of vehicles on the road, Tamil Nadu ends up with an unusually high level of overall traffic exposure.

Total Road Network Length & Density by State

Source: MoRTH Basic Road Statistics of India 2019–20, as of 31 March 2020. Density = total road length ÷ state area. Tamil Nadu is above the national average (~193) but not the single highest — Bihar and West Bengal score higher due to dense rural-road networks in smaller states.

Registered Motor Vehicles by State

Source: MoRTH Road Transport Year Book, as of 31 March 2015. Tamil Nadu has India's 2nd-largest vehicle fleet.

Put together, a dense-but-not-extreme road network, a huge vehicle fleet, and the resulting traffic exposure — population density × road length × vehicle volume — explain Tamil Nadu's cumulative fatality numbers far better than any single factor taken on its own.

Why do accidents gradually increase as day turns to night?

Chart coming soon — will visualize accidents by lighting condition and hour-of-day using granular timestamped data

Several factors likely compound as daylight fades. Reduced visibility after dark makes it harder to judge distances, spot pedestrians, and react in time, especially on roads with poor lighting or markings. The evening period also overlaps with rush-hour commute traffic plus leisure and social travel, pushing more vehicles onto the road at the same time drivers are most fatigued from a full day.

Night-time hours are also when drunk driving rates tend to be highest, and when enforcement — traffic policing, speed checks, sobriety checks — is typically sparser than during the day. Together, lower visibility, higher traffic density, driver fatigue, alcohol involvement, and lighter enforcement point in the same direction: more risk as the day turns to night.
